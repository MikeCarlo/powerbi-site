It lets you describe an application, and get a real one — running on Fabric, inheriting your existing governance and security. This episode is the first full pass at what that means for report builders, for BI teams, and for the awkward question of who maintains the thing after you build it.

## News & Announcements

- **[Rayfin on Microsoft Fabric](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-fabric/features/rayfin)** — Microsoft's official landing page for Rayfin, an open-source SDK that gives you a fully managed, code-first backend on Fabric. Database, authentication, and hosting come preconfigured, so you write application logic instead of infrastructure. Apps built this way inherit the governance, security, and compliance already established across your data estate.

- **[awesome-rayfin](https://github.com/microsoft/awesome-rayfin)** — Microsoft's curated gallery of community templates and resources. It ships nine starter templates you can scaffold with the Rayfin CLI, from a blank app to a field technician app to a Power BI fixer. It also documents the core concepts — data modeling, authentication, and typed data access — which is the fastest way to understand how a Rayfin project is put together.

## Main Discussion

**Topic:** What Rayfin is, and what it changes for people who build in Power BI

Rayfin is not a new visual or a new pane. It is a way to build a real web application whose backend lives inside Fabric, published as a Fabric item alongside your other artifacts. Mike has been building apps on Fabric since January by hand; the difference is that Rayfin removes the parts nobody wanted to own — auth, storage, deployment — and hands them to you preconfigured. Both hosts spend most of the episode circling the same question: if describing an app is now enough to get one, what exactly is the job?

- **The backend is the product.** Rayfin's value is not the front end you can see — it's that security, semantic models, SQL databases, and the OneLake platform underneath are already wired together. You are not standing up authentication. That is the part Mike keeps returning to.

- **Model quality decides your results.** Mike found the lower-tier models could not hold a Rayfin build together, while the stronger reasoning models understood how to structure the app. If you try this with whatever model is cheapest, you will conclude Rayfin doesn't work when the model was the problem.

- **A style guide is now a prerequisite.** Without a framework for visuals, formatting, and layout, the agent produces something that runs but looks unconsidered. Design the style guide first and the output improves immediately — that prep work moved from optional to load-bearing.

- **It fixes the table and matrix complaints directly.** Every limitation Tommy has hit with expanding columns, conditional visibility, and bookmark gymnastics becomes a sentence you type. Mike's blunt version: he doesn't want to build Power BI Desktop reports anymore.

- **The semantic model becomes more important, not less.** A lake house full of tables carries no business context, and neither agents nor people can infer how they relate. Mike's argument is that Databricks and Snowflake are adding semantic layers for exactly this reason — the model is what makes any of this answerable.

- **The handoff is the unsolved problem.** Mike can ask the agent to write documentation for the next agent, but if the client isn't on GitHub and isn't using agents, they cannot pick the project up. Tommy names the risk plainly: if the one person who built it is gone, who owns it?

- **This is a squeeze on Power Apps.** Different licensing, more cost, and now a faster path to the same outcome sitting right next to it in Fabric. Neither host was reaching for Power Apps before, and they are reaching for it less now.

- **The skills that matter shift toward communication.** Less hands-on-keyboard DAX, more articulating the problem, understanding what the business actually needs, and turning that into something buildable. Tommy's framing is that soft skills are what make the hard skills work here.

### Who should touch this first

Both hosts land in the same place on rollout: give Rayfin to your strongest Power BI developer — the one who understands the data *and* the people — and give them some AI training if they haven't done this before. Handing it to everyone at once produces ambiguity and slop. The harder problem is organizational: dedicating someone to research and prototype for half a week is a real cost, and most businesses struggle to justify it against a maybe.

### What it means for consultants

Mike's take on pricing is that speed is the product. If you can compress months into a week, you price for the outcome and the urgency, not the hours — and you stop competing on being the cheapest shop. Tommy raises the token-cost question that follows: on a Rayfin build, whose budget covers the agent? For now both treat it as a client cost, but it is a line item that did not exist on a Power BI engagement a year ago.
