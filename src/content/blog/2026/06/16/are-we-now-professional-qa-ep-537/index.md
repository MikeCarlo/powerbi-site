The volume of work coming out of a BI team is going up while the hours spent typing it out go down. That leaves a role neither host is entirely comfortable naming: you are increasingly the person who reviews the output rather than the person who makes it. This episode is an honest argument about what that requires, where the fundamentals still matter, and how the career ladder survives when the bottom rung is automated.

## News & Announcements

- **[Skills for Fabric](https://github.com/microsoft/skills-for-fabric)** — Microsoft's repository of reusable AI assistant instructions for working with Fabric, covering workloads, APIs, and best practices. They install as plugin bundles through the GitHub Copilot CLI or compatible editors including Claude, Cursor, and Windsurf. The skills cover warehouse work, Power BI authoring, lakehouse development, and migration — which is exactly the "bake senior knowledge into something reusable" pattern the hosts spend the back half of the episode arguing for.

## Main Discussion

**Topic:** The developer role is shifting from writing to reviewing — and what that costs

Mike's framing is that we still need developers; we are just asking them for something different. The direct work is shrinking, the review work is growing, and the crux of the episode is whether "reviewer" is a real job or a quietly diminished one. Tommy pushes back throughout — not on whether it's happening, but on what you have to know to do it well.

- **You cannot review what you don't understand.** Tommy's sharpest point: if you don't know that many-to-many relationships cause trouble, you will never think to ask about them. General questions like "what are you doing here?" don't surface the problems that matter. The fundamentals stay necessary even when you never type them.

- **The Italian analogy does the heavy lifting.** Ask an agent for a governance doc in Italian and you'll say "looks good, looks Italian." A senior Italian writer asks which region it's written for. That gap — noticing nuance you weren't looking for — is what separates reviewing from rubber-stamping.

- **Conceptual knowledge beats syntax knowledge now.** Mike no longer values being able to write a pure Python function unaided. He does still need to know what a notebook is, how authentication generally works, and enough structure to ask the agent the right thing.

- **Your MCP server is your junior developer.** Tommy names the problem directly: juniors became seniors by doing the tedious work — writing the measures, building the models, sitting through requirements. If the agent does all of that, the path up disappears.

- **Seniors should be writing skills, not just doing the work.** Mike's answer to the ladder problem is to bake senior judgment into reusable skills and harnesses. Knowledge transfer stops being a call between two people and becomes something the whole team inherits.

- **This is the Power Query jump again, only bigger.** Both hosts remember hand-engineering data in Excel before Power Query landed. The work didn't disappear — it moved up a level and made them more strategic. Mike thinks the MCP-and-agent shift is a larger version of the same move.

- **Hire juniors for communication and discernment.** Define a problem well, listen to the business, translate it into engineering terms. Mike half-jokes that the useful degree here is closer to sociology than business school — and that he wants evidence you're a genuine learner with real AI practice.

- **Trust is Tommy's closing separator.** A stakeholder has to be able to trust what the senior tells them. You can arrive at an answer through prompting and review, but you still have to know whether it's right.

### The dark room

Mike's recurring image for this moment is walking into a dark room and slowly turning on the lights — we can see a little further each week, but nobody knows the shape of the space yet. Neither host will predict which jobs exist on the other side. What they will say is that the upheaval is not limited to report authoring: data engineering and business intelligence as whole disciplines are built on people spending time making things, and that assumption is the one being removed.

## Looking Forward

Pick one thing you currently review by instinct and write it down as a skill your team can reuse — that's the senior knowledge that otherwise leaves when you do.
