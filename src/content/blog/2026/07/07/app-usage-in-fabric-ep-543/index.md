Both hosts have been on the wrong side of that question, and this episode is about the metrics and the process that keep you off it.

## Main Discussion

**Topic:** Measuring app and report usage in a way that actually means something

Mike opens with the memory that shaped his thinking: realizing that if his boss asked what value he'd delivered that year, "I created 35 reports" was the whole answer — and he had no idea whether anyone opened them. Tommy's contribution is a definition of *reliable* that puts trust and confidence at the center.

- **Usage numbers only mean something next to the business metric.** You cannot judge a sales app by opens alone. You need the sales team's calls and demos alongside it — if their numbers move and usage moves with them, you're supporting them. One side of the coin without the other proves nothing.

- **Active users over a rolling window, not raw opens.** Mike's default is 30 days; tighten to two weeks if you want to be critical. The question is whether people come *back*, not whether they visited once.

- **Tommy's benchmark for an org-wide app:** roughly 80% of the intended audience should touch it within a two-week window. Anything less and you're carrying reports that are quietly dead.

- **Reconcile your reports against the operational system.** Whatever you're reporting on should match what production says. It's basic, and it's the foundation of the trust Tommy is describing.

- **"If a report isn't making or saving money, kill it."** A callback to a position they took years before Fabric existed, and it still holds. The hard part is quantifying which decision a report supported and what that decision was worth.

- **An org app needs a stated goal before you publish it.** An organizational app is different from a pile of reports in a workspace — it affects the whole business, so it needs a target number attached to it from the start.

- **Write down why the report exists.** A single sentence on the report itself, stating its purpose. It aligns the page count, the measures, and the eventual usage conversation, and it gives you the baseline for measuring anything.

- **Plan for sunsetting.** Junk accumulates in every organization — that's not a failure, it's inevitable. What matters is having a process for identifying what isn't used and turning it off.

### Centralized, business-led, or federated

The right usage metrics depend on which structure you're running. A full center of excellence builds everything centrally and distributes it. A business-led model pushes creation outward. The middle path both hosts favor is federation — find the sharp person on the ops team who understands modeling, give them more exposure, and let them own content for their department.

### Scorecards close the loop

Tommy's point on this is that a number has to make someone happy or sad — it has to invoke something. A scorecard puts that number in one place a person actually looks. Mike ties it back to his own GitHub spend: agents mean far more commits and far more build minutes, and the cost only becomes manageable when someone sees their own figure on a scorecard rather than an aggregate nobody owns.
