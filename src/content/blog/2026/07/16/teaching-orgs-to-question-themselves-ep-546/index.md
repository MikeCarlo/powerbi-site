A Nightingale article about students reading graphs opens onto the real problem: we teach people to use Power BI, but not to interrogate what they're looking at — or to notice when they only interrogate the charts they dislike. Both hosts have watched a correct report get rejected in a meeting, and this episode is about why that happens and what to do differently.

## News & Announcements

- **[We taught students to read graphs but we forgot to teach them to question themselves](https://nightingaledvs.com/we-taught-students-to-read-graphs-but-we-forgot-to-teach-them-to-question-themselves/)** — The Nightingale piece driving the episode, from a professor who showed the same gas-price chart to two students and got two completely different reactions. It's the source of the term the hosts keep returning to: **motivated skepticism**, the mirror image of confirmation bias, where you scrutinize hardest the data that threatens you.

Also discussed: Fabric user data functions invoking the job scheduler through a service principal, Key Vault secret, and generic connection — very developer-centric, and both hosts agree it needs to exist even if a Power BI developer has no use for it today. Plus new AI-powered operations agents that watch pipelines and recommend fixes, which lands well after Mike spent half a day chasing a timed-out token through a pipeline.

## Main Discussion

**Topic:** Bias, trust, and what happens when a report threatens someone's job

- **Motivated skepticism is the concept to know.** Confirmation bias makes you accept what agrees with you. Motivated skepticism makes you demand sources, methodology, and caveats — but only for results you don't like. Same person, same chart, different standard of proof.

- **Tommy has lived it.** He brought a more accurate conversion number to a meeting, backed by Google's own documentation about sampled data, and watched it get rejected because it wasn't the number the team was used to seeing. Being right was not sufficient.

- **The y-axis decides the story.** Zoom in on a line chart and normal variation looks like chaos; zoom out and the same series is a steady climb. Mike uses the stock market as the example — the lens is a choice the author makes, whether or not they realize it.

- **Every red number is attached to a person.** A report showing a team down 10% arrives during their performance review year. Mike's headcount example is starker: an analysis showing too many middle managers is somebody's livelihood, and it cannot be wrong.

- **"Down" is not the same as "failed."** Sending fewer emails at lower spend with higher conversion isn't a loss. Without the surrounding dimensions, a downward line invites the wrong conclusion.

- **Segment the experiment from the baseline.** If leadership asked a team to try new messaging, flag that data separately. Otherwise the experiment drags the standard numbers down and the report looks like underperformance.

- **You mostly can't prevent this in advance.** Stakeholders won't mention the experiment because they don't think it's relevant. It surfaces when the chart is on screen — which is why the response matters more than the prevention.

- **Scorecards are for steering, not grading.** Tommy's favorite framing: goals on a report exist to tell you whether you're aligned while there's still time to adjust, not to deliver a verdict at the end of the campaign.

### Correlation is not the story either

The shark attacks and ice cream sales example gets its airing, and it connects to the episode's real warning: a chart with two lines that move together invites a causal reading the data doesn't support. Add a vertical annotation marking an event and you've made the suggestion explicit — which is exactly what the gas price chart in the article does.

### Empathy is part of the job

Mike's approach comes from presenting hard performance reports to customers: bring the reasoning, name the market dynamics, and build a story rather than a verdict. His AdWords example is the clearest case of bias in the wild — a client insisted a keyword performed because it showed up every time *they* searched it, while logged in and personalized. The technical explanation doesn't win that argument. Agreeing on the measure before the results arrive does.

## Looking Forward

Before your next review, agree with the stakeholder on what the measure means and what "on track" looks like — it's much easier than relitigating it after the chart is on screen.
